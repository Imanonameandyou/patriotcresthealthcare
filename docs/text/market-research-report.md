## PATRIOT CREST HEALTHCARE – MARKET RESEARCH REPORT
Title: Comprehensive Market Research: Private-Sector Health Insurance Plans & Consumer PsychologyPrepared for: Patriot Crest Healthcare (Independent 2-15 Health Insurance Agency, Florida)Date: November 16, 2026
## Executive Summary
Market Overview: The individual and family private insurance market includes ACA-compliant plans on and off the exchange (by carriers like Florida Blue, Aetna, Cigna, Oscar, Molina, Ambetter, etc.), as well as limited-benefit plans (short-term medical, fixed-indemnity, supplemental, health-sharing). Availability varies by state (e.g., no Kaiser in Florida), and plans range from guaranteed-issue ACA plans to medically underwritten private plans. Short-term plans (e.g. UHC/GoldenRule) offer brief coverage (1–12 months or up to 3 years with “TriTerm”)【40†L21-L24】 but exclude pre-existing conditions【40†L100-L104】.
Customer Segments: Key prospects include (A) self-employed/freelancers, (B) families without employer coverage, (C) individuals just out of employer plans, (D) people undergoing coverage transitions (job loss, divorce, aging out, COBRA expiration), and (E) early retirees/pre-Medicare. Each has distinct demographics, financial situations, frustrations, and goals.
Customer Psychology: Common threads are financial fear (premium spikes, deductibles, medical debts), frustration (complexity, surprise bills, narrow networks), and family responsibility (protecting loved ones, finding “peace of mind”). Prospects blame high costs on insurers and policy changes, and harbor misconceptions (e.g. “all plans cover pre-existing conditions” or “private plans are always cheaper”). Deeper desires include security and control over their healthcare.
Voice of Customer: Across Reddit and forums, prospects literally say things like: “It’s incredibly unfair…business owners have to provide it for themselves… It makes it hard to compete when I pay $30,000 more a year in premiums…It’s fucking bullshit.”【19†L121-L129】【19†L133-L136】 Another: “None of it’s decent…$400/mo for BlueCross Silver, pain to use, covers almost nothing…thinking about bronze and paying cash”【22†L350-L354】. Self-employed parents report paying ~$900/mo and $23K out-of-pocket for childbirth, then “said fuck it”【27†L553-L562】. Many veterans of ACA: “Qualifies at 106k for $334/mo bronze with $20K deductible, but at $107k it jumps to $1216…subsidy cliff is absurd.”【23†L119-L127】 A number resort to private plans to save money: “Got a private UHC PPO with no deductible…marketplace was $1600/mo. Only caveat: medical underwriting, but better coverage and rate.”【22†L390-L395】. These verbatim quotes show intense emotion — anger, frustration, relief — and the real language prospects use.
Existing Solutions: Customers compare ACA Marketplace plans (guaranteed-issue, includes essential benefits) with off-exchange ACA plans, employer coverage (if any), COBRA, short-term limited-duration plans (not ACA, pre-existing excluded), fixed-indemnity and supplemental plans, and faith-based health-sharing. Each has tradeoffs: ACA plans have predictable coverage but rising premiums (especially after 2025 subsidy cuts)【9†L75-L83】; short-term plans are cheaper but underwritten【40†L100-L104】; health-sharing may appeal to some values. We must clearly explain differences without overpromising (e.g. only ACA plans guarantee coverage for pre-existing conditions【40†L134-L142】).
Competitor Analysis: Other independent agencies (e.g., AffordableInsuranceTeam, AmericansFirstHealthcare) market heavily on “private market alternatives,” “no enrollment windows,” and “we shop all carriers for you.” They tout independence and multi-year plans (3-year stability)【29†L135-L143】【29†L197-L204】. They use prominent trust signals (BBB/Google reviews, media logos) and testimonials about saving money. Patriot Crest must similarly emphasize unbiased advice, carrier access (PPO/EPO plans nationwide), and a family-friendly, patriotic brand.
Objections: The most common barriers are “I can’t afford insurance or deductible,” “Is this even real insurance?” “What about pre-existing conditions?” “I might lose subsidy,” and “I hate pushy agents.” Research shows prospects fear buying a plan that leaves gaps. We must handle each objection with factual, empathetic answers (e.g. explain subsidies eligibility, private plans’ underwriting, ability to keep doctors) and reassure no pressure/no cost consultation.
Market Trends: Recent policy changes profoundly affect this market. The expiration of enhanced ACA subsidies (Jan 2026) caused a 10% drop in Florida enrollees【9†L75-L83】, with premiums up ~58%【9†L89-L93】. Nationally, marketplace enrollment declined. Meanwhile, short-term and private plans have been marketed as alternatives for those priced out. Telemedicine grew (VC coverable), some carriers are adjusting product offerings, and inflation has driven medical costs up. Florida’s individual market is large (several million) but volatile.
Marketing Angles: Emotional angles that resonate include “protect your family’s future,” “take control of skyrocketing costs,” and “don’t let COBRA or Obamacare fears trap you.” Financial angles: highlighting how a licensed agent can compare costs and coverage to find potential savings, or at least avoid nasty surprises. Family angles: caring for kids and spouse, freedom to visit any doctor. Self-employed angle: being treated “like a real person, not a corporate plan.” Trust angle: emphasizing independence (“works for you, not the insurance company”) and years of expertise. Educational angle: quiz-like “Are you overpaying?” or “Understand your coverage” hooks. Curiosity angles: e.g. “What big 3-year plan did USAA recently launch for agents?” or history of HSAs.
Customer Avatars: Four example personas emerge:

Find out if an alternative plan can save you.” Monitor legislative news (potential subsidy renewals, state actions). Finally, maintain compliance: no guarantees, no misrepresenting coverage scope.

## 1. Carrier & Product Landscape
Major Carriers: The private individual/family market is served by national insurers and regional carriers. In Florida, Florida Blue (BCBS of FL) is a dominant player on and off the ACA Marketplace. Other active carriers include Aetna, Cigna, Oscar, Molina, Ambetter (Centene), each offering ACA-compliant plans in Florida (with subsidies for eligible incomes). UnitedHealthcare recently re-entered the Florida market off-exchange, offering PPO products for the uninsured and short-term plans (through its Golden Rule subsidiary)【40†L21-L24】. Kaiser Permanente does not operate in Florida; it is limited to markets like CA, CO, etc. SelectHealth (a Utah Blue affiliate) and Anthem (California/KY etc.) are not Florida-specific, but nationally BCBS affiliates (Anthem, Anthem affiliates) sell ACA plans in several states. Oscar Health and Bright Health are smaller carriers with selective state presence.
Plan Types:
ACA-compliant major medical (on-exchange): Guaranteed issue, cover 10 EHBs, no pre-existing exclusions. After 2025, fewer carriers may offer these outside open enrollment. They come in metal tiers (Bronze/Silver/Gold).
Off-exchange ACA-like plans: Also fully insured, often similar networks/benefits but sold outside Marketplace (may not have subsidies).
Short-Term Medical Plans: Temporary coverage (1–12 months, up to 3 years with multi-term “TriTerm” plans【40†L21-L24】). Medically underwritten (pre-existing conditions not covered)【40†L100-L104】, no essential benefit requirements【40†L134-L142】. Carriers: UHC (Golden Rule), National General, Pivot Health, others.
Fixed-Indemnity/Supplemental Plans: Provide cash benefits or limited coverage for accidents, critical illness, dental/vision, but not major medical. (E.g., “limited-benefit” that advertise low premiums but pay limited dollar amounts.)
Health Sharing Ministries: Faith-based arrangements (e.g. Medi-Share, Sedera) – technically not insurance, mostly pre-existing excluded, use funds to pay members’ medical bills.
Association Group Plans: Occasionally available via business associations (e.g. chamber of commerce plans) with loose underwriting.
Distribution: Patriot Crest can offer any product for which it is appointed. Most carriers (UHC, BlueCross, Molina, Oscar, etc.) require agents to obtain a carrier appointment. The agent can present private off-exchange plans year-round (no SEP needed)【29†L135-L143】, whereas ACA marketplace plans require enrollment period or qualifying event. Agents must explain each product’s nature (e.g., short-term vs ACA) carefully to comply with regulations.
(No single source lists every plan; above summary is drawn from carriers’ product info and regulatory guidelines【40†L21-L24】【40†L100-L104】.)
## 2. Customer Segments
## A. Self-Employed Professionals and Business Owners
Profile: Independent contractors, freelancers, gig workers, small business owners (Owner often handles own insurance). Age 30–60, incomes variable (often $30K–150K). Usually ineligible for employer plans.
Frustrations: High premiums with no employer subsidy; unpredictable income and expenses make budgeting insurance hard【19†L121-L129】. Many have uninsurable or underinsured past experiences (e.g. “paid $30k/year”【19†L121-L129】). Dislike open enrollment constraints.
Motivations: They want flexibility and affordability. If healthy, they’re open to high-deductible/HSAs to reduce premiums, or even private plans to cut costs【22†L390-L395】. They often value being informed and having control (willing to shop around). A message like “find the plan that fits your real business needs” resonates.
Objections: “I can’t afford it,” “I just pay cash,” “Insurance is a scam” (some strongly say this【27†L553-L562】). They distrust agents and fear being upsold.
Key Angles: Emphasize tax deductions (they can deduct premiums), emphasize rolling with one’s income year-round. “We have solutions beyond $30k/year premiums”【19†L121-L129】.
## B. Families (Parents with Children)
Profile: Married/partnered with kids (often 1–3 children). Age 25–55. Middle-class incomes (e.g. $40K–100K). Might be dual earners or one non-working spouse.
Frustrations: Protecting children at reasonable cost. Stress over pediatric care, emergency room bills, or maternity benefits. Possibly lost employer coverage or on COBRA. Premiums may have been subsidized but are now rising dramatically【9†L75-L83】, causing anxiety.
Motivations: Peace of mind that family won’t face bankruptcy if something happens. Want clear value (e.g. “Will plan cover doctor for my kid’s asthma?”). Appreciate empathetic guidance (mom/dad seeing agent as advisor, not salesman).
Objections: “It’s too expensive,” “Our subsidy went away,” “Our agent from before only pushed the most expensive plan.” They also worry about losing existing doctors if switching plans.
Key Angles: Emphasize family security: “Coverage that fits your budget and still protects your children’s health.” Highlight pediatric/well-child visits if available. Use testimonials from other parents. Show scenarios of typical family savings or coverage.
## C. Individuals without Employer Coverage
Profile: Single individuals or couples age ~30–60. Could be young professionals, recently divorced, or just choosing to buy individually. Income 20K–80K.
Frustrations: Often new to shopping for insurance. Confusion over ACA vs. alternatives. May have basic knowledge of “Obamacare” but not specifics. High deductibles and co-pays deter them from seeing doctors.
Motivations: They want simplicity and affordability. If healthy and not poor enough for subsidies, they want to see if there’s a better plan than default ACA.
Objections: “Is this the same plan from healthcare.gov?” “If I get sick, will I lose everything?” “I heard companies deny claims.” They fear scams.
Key Angles: “Make sure you’re not overpaying for coverage you don’t need.” Offer a simple assessment. Reassure them about guaranteed coverage (if ACA) vs. savings (if private).
## D. Coverage Transitioners
Profile: People experiencing life changes: lost a job, employer stopped offering coverage, divorce, aging off parent plan (young adults), or change in income bracket. Age range varies (mid-20s to 60s).
Frustrations: Suddenly forced to find new coverage quickly. Confused by special enrollment rules. Emotional stress (job loss, divorce) compounds fear about health needs.
Motivations: Need immediate guidance. They value an agent who will “hold their hand.” They often have a fixed deadline (COBRA 18 months, divorce finalization).
Objections: “I don’t have time,” “I’ll just go uninsured,” “My doctor only accepts that old plan.” Some wrongly think they have no options off-season.
Key Angles: Highlight “outside open enrollment you still have options.” Provide clarity on special enrollment events. Emphasize speed & convenience (“we can find you something in days”).
## E. Early Retirees / Pre-Medicare
Profile: Age 50–64, retired or semi-retired, income may be lower. Possibly on long-term disability. Pre-existing conditions common.
Frustrations: Premiums skyrocket due to age. Many have chronic conditions (diabetes, hypertension). They fear moving into Medicare gap without coverage.
Motivations: Stability until 65. They often appreciate a patriotic framing (“veteran-owned business helping families”). Possible veteran status or union ties.
Objections: “No plan covers my condition,” “Medicare is years away, what do I do now?” They may have some awareness of ACA rights for pre-existing, but may doubt private plans.
Key Angles: Stress guaranteed-issue ACA if available (if in FL under 65 all pre-existing must be covered【40†L134-L142】). Point out any gap-year plans or special enrollment for retirement. Use gentle, respectful tone.
(These personas will guide copy tone and which benefits to highlight.)
## 3. Customer Psychology
## 1. Hopes & Dreams
Customers ultimately want security, control, and fairness. Top hopes include:
Financial stability: They dream of not fearing medical bankruptcy. “If everyone could afford doctor visits without worry…” (implied from quotes about spending vs cost【27†L553-L562】).
Family protection: “I just want my kids safe” is the underlying desire. Even self-employed clients say they would never risk their children’s health【22†L532-L536】.
Independence: Many self-employed see insurance choice as part of being their own boss, not reliant on an employer. They want options, like choosing their own coverage dates (no fixed open enrollment)【29†L135-L143】【29†L197-L204】.
Transparency and confidence: They want straightforward answers and to understand what they buy. Clients mentioned appreciation for clear communication (the competitor’s pitch “built around your health needs not your income”【29†L150-L158】 resonates with this).
## 2. Fears & Anxieties
Key fears include:
Unmanageable medical costs: People fear an unexpected illness wiping out savings. For example, one uninsured user faced $400k in hospital bills and “maxed out” her out-of-pocket, living on insurance only for life-threatening care【24†L94-L102】.
Being trapped in unaffordable premiums: After subsidies ended, many fear they can’t renew coverage (“I wanted to renew but new premium was $1,045 a month, higher than my mortgage”【9†L68-L73】).
Covered services not available: “Will my plan cover my kids’ specialists? My prescriptions?” Many mention surprise exclusions (e.g. childbirth not covered【19†L196-L200】).
Being misled: People worry agents may pressure them or hide costs. The FAQ in the competitor page tries to address “No catch” and “No pressure” explicitly.
Losing existing doctors: Changing plans can mean losing access to a trusted provider, a common hidden objection.
## 3. Frustrations
Frequent frustrations from research:
Skyrocketing premiums: “My premium doubled this year, with barely any changes in care【27†L612-L619】.”
High deductibles and copays: “$8,450 deductible, $9,200 OOP max, who has that lying around?”【25†L69-L77】
Complex jargon: “They throw acronyms at me, I feel lost when choosing plans” (implied).
Enrollment hassles: “I spent hours on healthcare.gov, then got a robot on the phone at Blue Cross”【27†L612-L619】.
Claim denials: Not directly cited, but a common frustration. They dread explaining and appealing claims.
## 4. Previous Victories (Positive Experiences)
Some express relief after a helpful experience:
People celebrate finding any plan after being uninsured or stuck. “Finally have something better than $23k out-of-pocket” (implied).
Testimonials praise when agents “explained everything clearly” (from competitor pages).
Some have switched to private plans and saved hundreds per month【22†L390-L395】, which gives them hope.
When a plan actually paid a claim (e.g., one person had successful shoulder surgery with short-term coverage【20†L175-L184】), they feel grateful.
## 5. Previous Failures (Pain Points)
Customers recount:
Bad plan choices: Many regret taking low-premium plans that had huge gaps (“Paid $900 premiums, still $23k for births, so we canceled”【27†L553-L562】).
Agent bait-and-switch: One user was convinced to sign up, then found services missing.
Assumptions proven wrong: People assumed emergency visits wouldn’t be that expensive, or that “if I have insurance they’ll cover it” (one learned that even great coverage can leave large bills【27†L553-L562】).
Missed subsidies: “Hit the subsidy cliff, had no clue”【23†L119-L127】.
## 6. Outside Forces & Blame
Many blame:
Insurance companies: “Greed of the health insurance industry”【27†L677-L681】.
Government policy: Lament end of subsidies【9†L75-L83】 or pre-existing rules. (“So glad we voted for the party that thinks preexisting means die”【25†L113-L122】, though this is partisan ranting).
Healthcare costs: Pharma, hospitals, general inflation: e.g., one comment notes “providers charge insane rates to insurers and then charge us the rest”【24†L169-L174】.
Health system complexity: “It’s just a messed up system, we’re incentivized to mooch off spouses”【19†L121-L129】.
Agents/brokers: Trust issues; they say “No pressure!” if agents are aggressive.
## 7. Misconceptions & Assumptions
Common beliefs include:
All plans cover pre-existing: false for private short-term and some private plans. We must correct that.
Lower premium = good deal: not true if deductible is huge (people are beginning to understand this).
Marketplace always best price: some discovered off-exchange/private was cheaper【22†L390-L395】.
Health-sharing equals insurance: not true; sharing has many limitations.
”I’m healthy so I don’t need insurance”: one user did avoid insurance and later faced $400k in bills【24†L98-L106】 – we should counter that “You buy insurance for a bad day, not today” argument.
## 8. Core Beliefs & Values
Responsibility to family: Protecting kids and aging parents is a core motivator. Fear of letting them down.
Fairness: “It should be universal for everyone”【19†L121-L129】. While they may not advocate single-payer, they do want fairness in who pays how much.
Independence: Valuing being self-reliant (self-employed) but feeling unfairly disadvantaged without employer.
Pragmatism: Many accept compromise (“I won’t do chemo if I have cancer” – a dark humor line【27†L551-L562】). They make cost-benefit judgments.
Patriotism/Local identity: As Patriot Crest brand suggests, an appeal to “support local, support veterans and small business” can resonate with Florida market sensibilities. (Not directly from quotes, but a known local cultural factor.)

## 8. Market Trends & Industry Intelligence
Enrollment & Subsidies: Nationwide ACA enrollment doubled from 2021–2025 under expanded subsidies【9†L82-L90】. However, subsidy expiry (end of 2025) caused sharp drops. Florida enrollment plunged ~10% (443K fewer people) between Feb 2025–26【9†L75-L83】. Florida avg. monthly premium (after subsidies) jumped 58%【9†L89-L93】 (from $67 to $106). Similar patterns hold nationally (source: CMS/AP News【9†L75-L83】). Agents should be aware that cost burdens are the top talking point now.
Premium Inflation: Medical inflation (~5-7%) plus market exit of some insurers has driven up premiums. Insurers actively seek to discourage highly sick enrollees (e.g. by raising rates). Reports (AP, CMS) indicate that in some markets, premiums increased 40–60% in 2026. Florida’s increase is notably severe【9†L89-L93】.
Carrier Participation: After the pandemic wave, some large carriers re-entered markets (e.g. UHC in FL possibly 2026–27). Others remain cautious. KFF analyses (2025) show insurer concentration rising – a few carriers dominate individual markets nationwide.
Regulatory Changes: There are no new mandates for 2027, but proposals to reinstate subsidies are pending. Florida did not expand Medicaid, leaving coverage gap people (too low for marketplace subsidy but ineligible for Medicaid). Florida’s individual market is thus bigger. New state regulations occasionally target short-term plan marketing (recent legislation: some states cap short-term renewals).
Consumer Behavior: KFF data shows many enrollees switching plans yearly; dissatisfaction is high. More people (especially millennials) use digital research before contacting agents. Online lead gen is effective. Word-of-mouth and online reviews increasingly influence choices.
State-Specific (Florida): As noted, Florida had ~3.85 million marketplace enrollees in Feb 2026 (of whom many are small group, some are likely short-term or grandfathered)【9†L75-L83】. Major Florida metros (Miami, Tampa, Orlando) have highest uninsured rates. Florida’s heavy retirement population means there’s also a large short-term market bridging to Medicare (55–64 age group).
Other Trends: Telemedicine benefits became more common (especially post-COVID). HSAs have higher limits now – we should mention them as a savings tool. Some insurers now allow year-round enrollment for off-exchange products. Also, interest in “silver loading” and “add-ons” can affect plan attractiveness.
(Sources: Florida Independent/News【9†L75-L83】, plus general knowledge of CMS and KFF market summaries.)
